# A reason for the color, not a default look

Conditional project method, not a new scientific law or a learned global taste. Recent paired tests exposed disagreement between model critique and user choice, unexplained surface tints, and revisions that lost useful relationships. These motivate the following hypotheses to test on new briefs; they do not establish that any hue, style or model is generally better.

## 1. Declare a compact color intention before choosing a palette

After domain dispatch, write: **context** (medium, audience, task/story), **intended reading**, **first focal/action region**, **supporting regions**, and **spatial intent** when relevant. Name up to three operative principles and what each changes in this case. Retrieve more evidence only for a relevant uncertainty or conflict; do not stack every available rule into the image prompt.

For each substantial surface tint or dominant color, state its job: semantic role, narrative emphasis, material/illumination, or evidenced brand context. An adjective such as “sophisticated” alone is not a reason for a beige or blue wash. For functional UI, compare an unexplained tinted surface with a neutral candidate before adopting it; preserve required status and action meaning. For art, neutrality is not the default goal: a deliberately colored world or light source can be the intention. This is neither a ban on tints nor a preference for neutral, blue, green or beige palettes.

## 2. Make one diagnostic correction, not a global cleanup

Before revising, record **target**, **relationships to preserve**, and **likely tradeoff**. Examples of protected relationships are a face-to-hand action, a material distinction, or foreground/background separation. Quieting a competing reflection does not require desaturating the whole image. Muting, lightening and desaturation are not automatic improvements. Reduce the prompt to the requested correction and preserved relationships; do not add unrelated art-direction rules during an edit.

Compare actual before/after pixels at the same scale, including the focal detail and a region the edit should not change. Ask the independent reviewer to name both improvement and regression, not merely award a higher score. Retain the earlier version when the correction materially weakens a required relationship without compensation; if no defect warrants a change, keeping it is a valid outcome. In a fixed-budget experiment, preserve both candidates and apply the same retention rule to every arm.

## 3. A critic is evidence, not the user's taste oracle

Keep objective/semantic failures, source fidelity, visual interpretation and preference separate. A higher aesthetic score cannot establish that the user will prefer an image. When reviewers or a user disagree, record the specific visible tradeoff and uncertainty rather than inventing a consensus or retroactively changing scores. Do not infer the cause of a user's choice when they gave no reason. Learn their exact feedback only within its actual scope; an interface reservation is not an instruction to remove atmospheric color from illustration.

## 4. Keep the source-fidelity promise honest

An image-edit prompt asking to “lock the line layer” does not actually lock it. Image generation can redraw, add detail, or weaken contours while improving color. Inspect source and output, including small objects and distant outlines; report concrete drift separately from color quality. Exact preservation requires registered source/contour evidence, not a 3/4 visual impression. If that evidence is absent, mark exact preservation unverified; if drift is observed, mark that requirement failed. Do not accept an exact-preservation claim on aesthetic scores. Offer the approximate study as such only within the user's allowed scope, or use a genuinely constrained fill/layer workflow in a separately authorized task.

## 5. Contextual harmony and storytelling are the creative target

For a creative request, technical correctness is a floor, not the finish line. Choose colors that fit the particular story, audience and use, then make their relationships convincing in the actual composition. Do not substitute a technically correct ramp, complementary-wheel recipe or persuasive explanation for a successful application.

Use a compact **context-to-color contract** before generating:
- **Communication:** one intended reading, two supporting qualities and one reading to avoid. Make these specific enough to distinguish the brief from a neighbouring use case; “beautiful and professional” is not diagnostic.
- **Context:** medium, audience and viewing/use conditions; separate supplied facts from reversible assumptions. Research culturally consequential symbolism rather than assigning a population a universal taste.
- **Relationships:** name the dominant field, supporting family and focal interruption, their approximate relative areas, adjacency and lightness/chroma relationships. Explain what unifies them and where deliberate tension serves the story. Harmony need not mean quiet, pastel, analogous or low contrast. Proportions are a starting hypothesis, not a 60–30–10 law.
- **Alternatives:** create two genuinely distinct color strategies, not adjacent hex substitutions. State the tradeoff in meaning or emphasis. Hold composition, copy, geometry and lighting fixed when comparing color judgement, unless the brief explicitly needs a coupled change; label the confound if they vary.
- **Applied proof:** judge color at its real size, placement and neighbouring colors. Check required functional roles/states separately. Equal swatches are not enough. Use the existing independent live-visual review, not code-only aesthetic verdicts.

Before reading the author's rationale, the independent reviewer records the perceived story, attention order, relationships that belong together and any competing color. Then reveal the brief and judge fit. Finally read the rationale to check whether it describes the visible result, rather than letting eloquent prose rescue a weak palette. Revise a diagnosed relationship and preserve successful ones. An unexpected interpretation can be useful evidence; it is not proof that the viewer is wrong.

Report **brief fit**, **relational harmony**, **attention hierarchy**, **appropriate distinctiveness**, **functional checks** and **personal preference** separately. A coherent palette can tell the wrong story; an intentionally tense palette can fit perfectly. A model reviewer is a development aid, not independent evidence of human comprehension. Consult `evals/contextual-harmony/protocol.json` for a candidate evaluation; until output and audience observations exist, do not claim this guide improves taste.

For a diagnosed pair or focal-to-ground issue, use `pair-judgement` and `docs/pair-judgement-method.md`. It adds separate whole-combination and named-foreground preference questions, not a new aggregate beauty score.

## Evidence and limits

This method operationalizes existing `convictions`, `meaning-depth-method`, domain contracts and the project's observed revision/communication failures. It adds no causal color-psychology claim, numerical beauty metric, automatic preference learning, or guarantee of unbiased review. The host must actually perform these comparisons; a guide and passing documentation tests cannot prove improved output.
