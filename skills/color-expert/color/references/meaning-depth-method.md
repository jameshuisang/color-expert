# Meaning without flattening the picture

Project operating principles and testable design hypotheses, grounded in `color-meaning-evidence`, existing `convictions` and `light-shadow`. They are not universal aesthetic laws or inferred permanent preferences.

## M1. Specify the intended reading before naming a hue
**When:** a brief asks for joyful, solemn, calm or energetic color.
**Do:** state audience, context and desired interpretation; distinguish “looks calm,” “I like it,” and “makes me feel calm.” Offer a clear direction with a falsifiable visual prediction, not a psychology slogan.
**Example:** a red accent can support an affectionate invitation or a destructive-action warning. Wording, icon, placement and conventions change interpretation even when the red stays fixed.
**Check:** show actual context, ask intended viewers what it communicates before revealing the intended emotion. Keep interpretation and liking as separate questions.
**Limit:** the example is a design hypothesis, not a causal emotional treatment.

## M2. Use population associations as hypotheses, not audience stereotypes
**When:** cultural or geographic symbolism matters.
**Do:** consult relevant local conventions and intended users; preserve ambiguity when evidence is absent. Do not assign a person's reaction from nationality, language or appearance. Shared patterns and individual variation can both be real.
**Example:** do not label white universally joyful or universally mournful; a specific ceremony needs specific context.
**Check:** record whose interpretation was actually tested and how. Do not convert country-level correlations into individual predictions.

## M3. Quiet is not synonymous with equal values or uniformly low chroma
**When:** a quiet illustration also requires a focal destination and spatial depth.
**Do:** keep a restrained palette while preserving differences between front/middle/rear planes, focal light and supporting masses. Protect depth cues before reducing every region's contrast. A flat treatment may be intentional; ask whether spatiality is part of the brief.
**Example:** an illuminated pavilion can remain warm and bright while an interior/rear plane is darker and cooler. This is one possible authored cue, not a law that distance is always blue or shadows always cool.
**Check:** compare the same geometry with one changed tonal relationship. Inspect full scene and local adjacencies; judge depth, focal destination and atmosphere separately. Do not maximize all contrast or “fix” flattening by increasing every saturation.
**Evidence type:** synthesis from contextual-color and light/shadow mechanisms; not proven causation from the paired baseline.

## M4. Critique the regression introduced by a correction
**When:** revising an image to reduce competition or improve harmony.
**Do:** retain a before/after reference; name what must not be lost. A lighter background may quiet clutter but also erase the pavilion's distinction. Ask the independent reviewer which relationship improved and which weakened.
**Check:** keep geometry, viewing scale and brief fixed. Grade depth and fidelity separately from beauty/liking. Report tradeoffs rather than averaging away a serious failure.

## M6. Visual interest is not maximum saturation, detail or contrast
**When:** a coherent image feels dull, or a reviewer calls for “more interest.”
**Do:** ask what should reward looking: a focal story, a secondary discovery, a rhythmic variation or material/light distinction. Propose one purposeful variation and preserve quiet support. Do not use a global colorfulness/entropy score as the target.
**Check:** independent viewers describe focal order and worthwhile secondary details before seeing the intent. Record liking separately from interestingness and from task completion.
**Limit:** research on photographic interest does not prescribe an illustration recipe. A spare, flat icon can fulfill its brief without rich depth or detail.
